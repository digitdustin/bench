What the data says

Findings

  1. 1

    You write in lowercase and stop without punctuation

    92% of typed Claude Code prompts start lowercase and 77% end with no punctuation at all. You end with a period 5% of the time. I end with one 94% of the time.

  2. 2

    Your register shifts by audience, mostly in capitalization

    In Slack about half your messages start with a capital, and in threads with execs it rises to 70%. Apostrophes stay dropped everywhere (dont, im, thats, about 9 to 1 against keeping them).

  3. 3

    Dictation looks like a different person

    Wispr messages are capitalized, fully punctuated, and keep apostrophes. The skill should learn word choice from them but never punctuation.

  4. 4

    You ask, I declare

    You use 13 question marks per 1,000 words, and I use 1. You hedge with i think and maybe and close with no? or or no. I open with labels like Short answer: and announce counts (Two things: opens 33 of my sentences).

  5. 5

    Your punctuation joins ideas, mine builds structure

    You join ideas with / , + and a spaced hyphen. I use 4x your colons, 28x your semicolons, bold on almost every reply, arrows, and the X, not Y contrast at 7x your Slack rate. You have never typed an em dash in this corpus.

Rules the skill takes from this

  • Short Slack replies are one line, 3 to 8 words, no ending punctuation.
  • Capital at the start is a coin flip in Slack, mostly lowercase in DMs, usually capital with execs. Mid-message i stays lowercase about 60% of the time.
  • A question gets a ?. Statements end bare. Never a closing period on a one-liner.
  • Paragraph breaks over long sentences. About a quarter of your longer prompts are multi-paragraph, one idea per block.
  • Emoji go at the end of a line, one at most, and only in Slack.

Typos: measured, not copied

Your typed prompts have real typos, mostly transposed letters (cna, abotu, hwo, htese, liek) and misplaced spaces (weirdlyi n, ont he). Fake typos read as fake fast. The proposal is to keep them off by default and allow only the ones that are really a style choice: dropped apostrophes, tho, pls, stretched words like muchhhh.

Layer 2, structure

How you build a message

Separate from punctuation: the order you put ideas in, how you hedge, how you join clauses. These shapes come up again and again.

Ask, then the context

can you show a loading indicator in the background when the stream is loading. i like the fade in when it loads, but i wanna know its loading

The request comes first and the reason follows as a plain clause. can you opens 67 of 344 prompts, and i want or we should covers most of the rest.

Opinion, softened, then an invitation to disagree

remove the scope to do the dialog to cancel limit order - it should just cancel the order no? i dont think theres much concern here but feel free to push back

Tag questions (no?, or no, right?) and i think (25 times in prompts, 22 in Slack) carry a firm view without sounding final.

Slash and plus as conjunctions

at the very least a more balanced / perspective shift on the sneaks would be good

should show candidate names + images

/ means "or, roughly". + means "and, together". In Slack you use them 4 to 5x as often as I do.

Short verdicts, all lowercase

thats not a very good check

it needs work. UX and visually, etc. but nothing crazy major

Judgments are short and plain, and often trail off with etc (29 times in prompts).

The proposal

How the skill works

Name: dustin-voice. It triggers when you ask for a draft you'll send as yourself ("draft a reply", "write this for slack", "say this how i would") and stays out of artifacts, docs and code comments unless you ask. It only drafts. It never sends.

~/.claude/skills/dustin-voice/
├── SKILL.md                 trigger, workflow, the hard rules (about 120 lines)
├── references/
│   ├── registers.md         one profile per scenario: the Layer 1 numbers as targets
│   ├── structure.md         Layer 2 shapes, each with 2 to 3 real examples
│   ├── lexicon.md           your words, words to avoid, swaps (serves as -> is)
│   ├── ai-tells.md          the Layer 4 field guide with rewrite moves
│   └── examples/            60 to 80 real messages per register, names scrubbed
├── scripts/
│   ├── lint_voice.py        scores a draft against a register and flags tells
│   └── refresh_corpus.py    rebuilds stats + examples from transcripts and Slack
└── corpus/                  local only, gitignored: raw extracts + stats.json
  1. 1

    Pick the register

    From where the text is going and who's reading it: Slack DM, group DM, channel post, exec thread, PR comment, or a doc in your voice. If it isn't clear, it asks once.

  2. 2

    Write the content

    Work out what to say before applying any style: the ask, the facts, the one opinion. It aims for your length for that register, so a DM reply is 4 to 8 words.

  3. 3

    Render it in your voice

    Apply the register's casing and punctuation, your openers and connectors (/, +, - ), hedges where you'd hedge, and pull 5 matching real examples from examples/ as few-shot.

  4. 4

    Lint

    lint_voice.py blocks em dashes, semicolons, bold, headers, banned words and contrast frames. It warns on closing periods, kept apostrophes in casual registers, Unicode arrows, counted lists, threes, and length more than 2x your median. The draft is rewritten until there are no blocks.

  5. 5

    Show the draft

    One draft, plus a shorter alternative when the register allows it. You edit or send. Your edits can be saved as new examples, so it learns from corrections.

What it considers

  • Audience seniority (exec threads: more capitals, fewer jokes, still no periods)
  • Channel vs DM vs thread reply
  • Message job: ask, update, feedback, pushback, ack, announcement
  • Your length distribution for that job
  • Whether a list is warranted (only channel posts with 3+ items)
  • Emoji: Slack only, at most one, from your actual set

How we'll know it works

  • A blind test: 10 real messages mixed with 10 skill drafts on the same prompts. It passes if you can't beat 65% at telling them apart.
  • Drafts score inside your own range on every Layer 1 measure for the target register.
  • Zero hard tells across a 30-draft eval set.
  • Re-run refresh_corpus.py monthly so it tracks how your writing changes.
